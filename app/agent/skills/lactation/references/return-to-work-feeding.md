# Return to Work Feeding

Help the mother find a realistic, sustainable balance among work, rest, and feeding goals after returning to work.

## Management Framework

Returning to work does not mean she must reproduce her previous feeding routine exactly. Instead, help establish a new balance of supply, demand, and caregiving that fits her goals and circumstances.

Consider these dimensions:

1. **Mother's feeding goals**
   - Whether she wants to continue exclusive breastfeeding, mixed feeding, or gradually reduce breast milk;
   - Whether she wants to maintain current supply or accepts some change after returning to work;
   - Whether supply, the direct breastfeeding relationship, rest, or convenience at work matters most to her.

2. **Working conditions**
   - Work hours and commute;
   - Whether there are predictable breaks and a suitable place to pump;
   - The pumping opportunities she can realistically use;
   - Whether milk needs to be carried, refrigerated, or transported.

3. **Baby's daytime feeding arrangements**
   - Who cares for the baby during the day;
   - Whether the baby receives expressed milk, formula, or both;
   - Whether she continues direct breastfeeding when at home;
   - How the baby is adapting to bottles and feeding by another caregiver.

4. **Current lactation situation**
   - Breastfeeding and pumping pattern before returning to work;
   - Whether supply has become relatively stable;
   - Any marked change in supply, engorgement, or pumping discomfort since returning.

Ask only for information that affects the plan. Do not require a full work and feeding timetable upfront.

## Service Decisions

**The aim is a feeding arrangement the mother can sustain over time, not a theoretically ideal plan she cannot follow in practice.**

Choose the next step based on her circumstances:

- **She wants to maintain breastfeeding as much as possible**
  → Build around feasible milk-removal opportunities during work and preserve direct breastfeeding when mother and baby are together if convenient.

- **Work conditions are restrictive**
  → Do not insist on a fixed number of pumping sessions. Find the most realistic opportunities, then adjust to supply and physical response.

- **Supply appears to have fallen markedly after returning to work**
  → Load `milk-supply-assessment.md` as needed to assess whether supply and demand have actually changed; if she wants to increase or maintain supply, then load `milk-supply-management.md`.

- **Pumping at work is painful or ineffective**
  → Load `pumping-support.md` and prioritize comfort and effective milk removal.

- **Frequent engorgement, lumps, or pain after returning**
  → Load `breast-symptoms.md`; do not rely only on extra pumping or repeatedly emptying the breasts.

- **The current approach feels too exhausting**
  → Consider a less intensive goal, a different pumping arrangement, or mixed feeding. Do not assume exclusive breastfeeding is the default goal.

Consider work demands, sleep, commute, family support, and her own feeding goals together.

## Designing the Plan

Build the return-to-work plan around three parts of the day:

1. **Before work**
   - Decide whether to breastfeed or pump based on the mother and baby's usual habits.

2. **During work**
   - Pump during breaks that are actually feasible;
   - Aim for comfort, sustainability, and effective milk removal rather than rigid times.

3. **After work**
   - Return to direct breastfeeding, bottles, or a combination according to their needs;
   - Do not add a substantial burden just to “make up” for daytime sessions.

If supply needs adjustment, adapt the plan to actual trends rather than setting a long-term fixed schedule all at once.

## Response Guidance

Center the response on three questions:

1. **What does the mother most want to preserve after returning to work?**
2. **What feeding and pumping opportunities are realistically available?**
3. **What arrangement is easiest to sustain?**

Prioritize one to three important adjustments at a time; do not mechanically produce a complete schedule.

If she needs specific times, develop them together using her working hours, commute, and the baby's feeding rhythm.

## Safety and Behavior

- If you identify an emergency medical risk, self-harm risk, or risk of harm to the baby or others, follow the shared safety guidelines first.
- Follow the shared behavioral guidelines throughout the service.
